The Willamette Resource Office reviews concrete mix designs for public building projects, looking for ways to lower a project's embodied carbon without missing its targets. Fly ash and slag are two materials that can replace part of the portland cement in a mix, and a design team has already set a target mass for the two combined and a target for the CO2 they may add together. Your job as a junior engineer on the office's software team is to write the two functions that work out how many kilograms of each material to use.

## How This Assignment Works

You work **test first**: you read the problem, decide what correct behavior looks like by writing your own test cases, plan the code in pseudocode, and only then write the code. The problem is solvable in Python or Rust, whichever you prefer. The two equations you need are given below in full, so you never derive or solve anything by hand; the work is turning them into clear, correct code. The order is fixed: design your tests, write your pseudocode, then write your functions. After that comes an optional extra-credit stretch, a short paper trace, and a two-sentence reflection.

## What to Submit

You submit in two places. Mechanics are in [Running and Submitting Your Code](/practicalities/starter-repo-and-check/); each section below also repeats what it asks for.

- **A single PDF on Canvas** holding everything that is not code: your **acceptance tests**, your **pseudocode**, your **paper trace**, and your **reflection**.
- **Your code on Gradescope**: the two functions `fly_ash_mass_kg` and `slag_mass_kg`, plus the extra-credit `first_material_mass_kg` if you attempt the summit.

## The Problem

Ordinary portland cement carries a heavy carbon cost: producing one metric ton releases about 919 kilograms of CO2 equivalent, cradle to gate (Portland Cement Association industry-average Environmental Product Declaration, certified by ASTM International, issued 2021). Fly ash and slag cost far less, because both are byproducts of another industry's process, a coal power plant and an iron blast furnace. Industry accounting for a byproduct only charges it for the energy and transport it takes to turn the raw byproduct into a usable material, not for whatever process originally produced it. Under that accounting, the Slag Cement Association's industry-average Environmental Product Declaration (certified by ASTM International) puts processed slag at about 147 kilograms of CO2 equivalent per metric ton. WRO's own lab testing of the fly ash sources the office uses puts that material at about 40 kilograms of CO2 equivalent per metric ton, in the same low range.

For a given project, the mix design team sets a target mass for fly ash and slag combined, and a target for the total CO2 the two of them may add together. Write <Latex formula="x" /> for the fly ash mass and <Latex formula="y" /> for the slag mass, both in kilograms; write <Latex formula="a" /> for fly ash's CO2 intensity and <Latex formula="b" /> for slag's, both in kilograms of CO2 per kilogram of material. Write <Latex formula="M" /> for the binder mass target and <Latex formula="C" /> for the total CO2 target, both already converted to the units the equations need. Two requirements become two equations: the two masses must add up to the target, and the CO2 each one adds must sum to the target:

<Latex formula="x + y = M" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="a\,x + b\,y = C" inline={false} />

WRO's engineers have already solved this pair of equations for the two masses:

<Latex formula="x = \frac{C - b\,M}{a - b}" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="y = M - x" inline={false} />

You will write two functions:

1. `fly_ash_mass_kg(binder_target_tonnes, co2_target_kgco2_per_tonne, flyash_kgco2_per_kg, slag_kgco2_per_kg)` returns <Latex formula="x" />, the fly ash mass in kilograms.
2. `slag_mass_kg(binder_target_tonnes, co2_target_kgco2_per_tonne, flyash_kgco2_per_kg, slag_kgco2_per_kg)` returns <Latex formula="y" />, the slag mass in kilograms.

Both return a float. The binder mass target arrives in tonnes, not kilograms, and the CO2 target arrives as a rate: kilograms of CO2 per tonne of binder, not a total amount. The two materials' intensities, `flyash_kgco2_per_kg` and `slag_kgco2_per_kg`, are already in kilograms of CO2 per kilogram of material. How you turn the arguments into <Latex formula="M" /> and <Latex formula="C" />, and what intermediate values you give names to along the way, is entirely your decision.

We give you **one** worked example to show the format. With `binder_target_tonnes = 8.0`, `co2_target_kgco2_per_tonne = 203.125`, `flyash_kgco2_per_kg = 0.125`, and `slag_kgco2_per_kg = 0.25`:

**Inputs and guarantees.** Every input is a float greater than zero. Fly ash's intensity and slag's intensity are always different, so `flyash_kgco2_per_kg - slag_kgco2_per_kg` is never zero and you do not have to guard against dividing by it. WRO only sets targets that are physically achievable, so both masses always come out positive.

## Summit Problem (extra credit)

This one is a real stretch, and it earns extra credit. It is also your practice for the graded stretch problem in the recitation room, where a harder problem is waiting.

Some mixes use a **third** material alongside fly ash and slag, and then a third requirement is added: the blend must also stay within a total materials cost budget. With material masses <Latex formula="x_1, x_2, x_3" />, CO2 intensities <Latex formula="a_1, a_2, a_3" />, per-kilogram costs <Latex formula="p_1, p_2, p_3" />, a binder mass target <Latex formula="M" />, a CO2 target <Latex formula="C" />, and a cost budget <Latex formula="P" />, the three requirements are three equations:

<Latex formula="x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = M" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="a_1 x_1 + a_2 x_2 + a_3 x_3 = C" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 + p_3 x_3 = P" inline={false} />

One standard way to solve a three-equation system like this is **Cramer's rule**, which is given here for you to implement. First, the value of a three-by-three determinant is:

<Latex formula="\det\begin{pmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{pmatrix} = a(ei - fh) - b(di - fg) + c(dh - eg)" inline={false} />

Let <Latex formula="\Delta" /> be the determinant of the grid of coefficients on the left-hand sides (`1, 1, 1` on the first row, the three CO2 intensities on the second, and the three costs on the third). Let <Latex formula="\Delta_1" /> be that same determinant but with the first column replaced by the three right-hand-side values <Latex formula="M, C, P" />. Then the first material's mass is:

<Latex formula="x_1 = \frac{\Delta_1}{\Delta}" inline={false} />

Write `first_material_mass_kg`, returning <Latex formula="x_1" />:

## Paper Practice

Trace this program by hand, on paper. It uses nested function calls, the pattern from the [Functions](/lectures/functions/) lecture. A stockpile of material is drawn down by two orders in a row. Build a table: for each call to `kg_remaining`, write the values its `total` and `used` parameters hold and what it returns; then, for `stockpile_after_two_orders`, write the values bound to `step` and to `result`. Write the exact value the program prints.

```python title="trace_me.py"
def kg_remaining(total, used):
    return total - used

def stockpile_after_two_orders(total, first_order, second_order):
    step = kg_remaining(total, first_order)
    result = kg_remaining(step, second_order)
    return result

answer = stockpile_after_two_orders(5000.0, 1800.0, 1450.0)
print(answer)
```

<Reveal label="Reveal the completed trace">
The call `stockpile_after_two_orders(5000.0, 1800.0, 1450.0)` runs `kg_remaining(5000.0, 1800.0)` first, which returns `3200.0` and is bound to `step`; then `kg_remaining(3200.0, 1450.0)`, which returns `1750.0` and is bound to `result`.

| Line | call | `total` | `used` | `step` | `result` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `step = kg_remaining(total, first_order)` | `kg_remaining(5000.0, 1800.0)` | `5000.0` | `1800.0` | `3200.0` | |
| `result = kg_remaining(step, second_order)` | `kg_remaining(3200.0, 1450.0)` | `3200.0` | `1450.0` | `3200.0` | `1750.0` |

The program prints `1750.0`. That trailing `.0` is Python's display of a whole-valued float; the same program written in Rust prints the `f64` as `1750`.

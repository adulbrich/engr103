The habitat's trans-Mars injection burn is the long engine firing that leaves Earth orbit and puts the crew on course for Mars. The flight computer needs three kinds of number before it can run the burn: how much mission time has passed, how fast the ship is going in units the cockpit can read, and how much propellant the burn needs once a safety margin is added. Your job as the new flight-software crew member is to write the functions that produce those numbers. HAB, the habitat's assistant, has already drafted one of the helpers; do not trust it until you have checked it yourself.

## How This Assignment Works

You write the functions named below and nothing else: the program's `main`, its input, its output, and the local test runner are already written, and the automated grader calls your functions directly with values it chooses, reading only what you `return`. Solve each problem in Python or Rust, whichever you prefer, and you may switch languages between problems; both are checked against the same acceptance cases. Submit your functions to the grader from the course starter repository. The assignment has four parts: the problem set below, a paper trace, a two-sentence reflection, and an optional summit problem.

## Problem Set

### Problem 1: The Mission Clock

**Context.** Mission Control tracks elapsed time as a whole number of seconds since launch. The crew display wants it broken into whole sols and the time of day within the current sol. A **sol**, one Martian day, is exactly `88775` seconds.

**Requirements.**

1. `whole_sols(seconds)` must return the number of whole sols that have fully elapsed in `seconds` seconds. A sol counts only once it is complete, so `88774` seconds is still zero whole sols.
2. `leftover_seconds(seconds)` must return the seconds elapsed into the current, not-yet-complete sol: the remainder left after the whole sols are removed.
3. `sol_hours(seconds)` must return the number of whole hours into the current sol. Take the seconds elapsed into the current sol, then divide by the `3600` seconds in an hour, discarding the minutes and seconds that do not fill a whole hour. This function combines both operators from the two above: the remainder that strips the whole sols, and the integer division that counts whole hours. The order in which you apply them decides the answer, so choose it deliberately.

The modeling step is deciding to represent mission time as whole sols, leftover seconds, and hours into the current sol; the analysis step is computing each with the integer-division and remainder operators from the [Expressions and Operators](/lectures/expressions-and-operators/) lecture, and composing the two of them for `sol_hours`.

<Reveal label="Reveal a nudge on HAB's mistake">

## Summit Problem (optional, worth nothing)

This one is a real stretch, and it earns no points; it is here so you can practice for the graded stretch problem in the recitation room, where a harder problem does count.

**The blend.** On approach, the crew mixes three propellant stocks into the reaction mass. Each stock carries three constituents, call them A, B, and C, in known proportions. Stock 1 has proportions <Latex formula="a_1, b_1, c_1"/>, stock 2 has <Latex formula="a_2, b_2, c_2"/>, and stock 3 has <Latex formula="a_3, b_3, c_3"/> (each a value between 0 and 1). You choose the blend fractions <Latex formula="M_1, M_2, M_3"/>, the share of the final mix drawn from each stock, and they must sum to 1. Your job is to find the blend fractions that make the final mix **balanced**: equal total proportions of A, B, and C.

**The math.** The balance conditions and the fact that the fractions sum to 1 give three equations in three unknowns. Solving that system by hand (substituting <Latex formula="M_3 = 1 - M_1 - M_2"/> and reducing) yields closed forms for each fraction. Writing <Latex formula="d = a_2 - a_3 + b_3 - b_2"/> for the shared denominator, the results are:

<Latex formula="M_1 = \frac{(c_3 - a_3) + (a_3 - a_2 + c_2 - c_3)\frac{b_3 - a_3}{d}}{(c_3 - a_3 + a_1 - c_1) + (a_3 - a_2 + c_2 - c_3)\frac{a_1 - a_3 + b_3 - b_1}{d}}" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="M_2 = \frac{b_3 - a_3 - M_1(a_1 - a_3 + b_3 - b_1)}{d}" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="M_3 = 1 - M_1 - M_2" inline={false} />

These expressions are long and easy to mistype, so build them from small pieces: compute <Latex formula="d"/> first, then each numerator and denominator on its own, exactly as the requirements name them.

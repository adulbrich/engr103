The Willamette Resource Office helps residents compare vehicles before they buy one. An electric vehicle costs more carbon to manufacture than a gasoline vehicle, mostly from its battery, but it emits less carbon on every kilometre it drives afterward. Residents keep asking the office how far they would need to drive an EV before that lower running cost makes up for the higher manufacturing cost. Your job as a junior engineer on the office's software team is to write a program that reads one vehicle's numbers from the console and prints how that payback plays out.

## How This Assignment Works

You work **test first**: you read the problem, decide what correct output looks like by writing your own test cases, plan the program in pseudocode, and only then write the code. You have not learned to write your own functions yet, so the contract is the program's **printed output**: your program reads four values from standard input and the automated grader compares the lines it prints to the expected lines. The problem is solvable in Python or Rust, whichever you prefer, against the same expected output. The order is fixed: design your tests, write your pseudocode, then write the program. After that comes an optional extra-credit stretch, a short paper trace, and a two-sentence reflection.

## The Problem

An electric vehicle's battery is the reason it costs more carbon to build. IVL's 2019 review of battery-production studies puts battery manufacturing at 61 to 106 kilograms of CO2 equivalent for every kilowatt-hour of battery capacity produced (IVL, *Lithium-Ion Vehicle Battery Production: Status 2019*). Once a vehicle is on the road, the EV usually wins the difference back, because it emits less carbon for every kilometre it drives than the gasoline vehicle it replaces. Burning gasoline releases about 2,348 grams of CO2 for every litre (EPA, calculated from the agency's cited figure of 8,887 grams of CO2 per gallon of gasoline burned). The EV's own operating emissions depend on the carbon intensity of the electricity grid it charges from, and two real grid figures give visibly different numbers: the Northwest Power Pool subregion, which covers most of Oregon and Washington, runs at 275 grams of CO2e per kilowatt-hour (EPA eGRID2022, released January 2024, rounded from 605.9 lb CO2e/MWh), while the United States' national average runs at 373 grams of CO2e per kilowatt-hour (EPA eGRID2022, rounded from 823.1 lb CO2/MWh). The office also keeps each vehicle's manufacturer-rated battery service life on file, typically 8 years or 160,000 kilometres, whichever comes first.

For every vehicle it evaluates, a field analyst has already turned figures like these into four numbers and hands them to your program through the console: how much extra carbon the EV's manufacturing cost over the gasoline vehicle's, in grams of CO2e; how many grams of CO2e the gasoline vehicle emits for every kilometre it drives; how many grams of CO2e the EV emits for every kilometre it drives; and how many kilometres a typical driver covers in a year.

**What your program must print.** Read the four values below from standard input, one whole number per line, in this order:

1. The EV's extra manufacturing footprint, in grams of CO2e.
2. The gasoline vehicle's operating emissions, in grams of CO2e per kilometre.
3. The EV's operating emissions, in grams of CO2e per kilometre.
4. The typical annual driving distance, in kilometres.

Then print exactly three lines, one whole number per line, in this order and nothing else on any line:

1. The payback distance: the whole number of kilometres the vehicle must be driven before the EV's lower running emissions have paid back its extra manufacturing footprint.
2. The number of complete years of the typical annual driving distance that fit within that payback distance.
3. The kilometres driven in the partial year left over once those complete years are accounted for.

Your program must not print anything before those three lines, including a prompt. Python's `input()` and Rust's `println!` do not add a newline to the screen the same way, so the same prompt would leave the two languages printing different text for what must be identical output; read every value with a bare `input()` call in Python or `read_i32()` in Rust, with no prompt text.

We give you **one** worked example to show the format. With an extra manufacturing footprint of `4800000`, a gasoline figure of `190`, an EV figure of `55`, and a typical annual distance of `15000`, the program prints:

```text frame="terminal" title="Output"
35555
2
5555
```

**Assumptions and edge cases.** Every input value is a whole number. The extra manufacturing footprint is at least `0` and at most `20000000`. Each per-kilometre figure is at least `1` and at most `1000`, and the EV's figure is always strictly less than the gasoline vehicle's, so the EV genuinely saves carbon on every kilometre driven. The typical annual distance is at least `1` and at most `100000` kilometres. An extra manufacturing footprint of `0` must produce three lines of `0`. The grader may choose any input values within these limits, so compute every printed number from the values you read and never hard-code a result such as `35555` or `2`.

AURA, the office's assistant, offers this: "The EV's own per-kilometre figure is the rate that matters here. Divide the extra manufacturing footprint by the EV's per-kilometre figure, and the result is your payback distance in kilometres. The gasoline vehicle's number is background information for the report, not part of the division." Test AURA's formula against the requirement above before you rely on it, and let your test cases settle the question.
</Aside>

The assignments are your take-home practice: the place to work through a problem with any tool you like, including AI, before a proctored recitation tests the same skeleton without them. They are low-stakes on purpose, and worth a small share of your grade. You work each one **test first**, the way real software is written: you read the problem, design your own test cases, plan the code in pseudocode, and only then write it. Your grade combines those parts, most of it for code that produces the right answers and the rest for the test design, the pseudocode, the paper practice, and the reflection you do along the way; every assignment's page carries its exact rubric. The point is still not the points. The point is that you arrive at each recitation having already built the thing it will ask you to build again.

Because they are the tier where AI is allowed, they are also the tier that trains you to **judge** code, not just produce it. That is where HAB comes in.

## Mission Ares

You are a member of the flight software crew of **Mission Ares**, the first crewed habitat on Mars. Across the term the assignments follow one mission, one episode at a time: preparing the vehicle, the launch, the burn that sends you to Mars, the long transit, the first days on the surface, and finally the Sol 100 status report that sums up everything the software has to get right. Each assignment opens with a mission beat that sets the scene and hands you a job.

Your crewmate on every assignment is **HAB**, the habitat's onboard AI. HAB is helpful, tireless, and, at exactly the wrong moment, confidently wrong. On each assignment HAB offers a **claim**: a confident, plausible statement about how to solve the problem, which is sometimes subtly wrong. Your job is to judge it and reject what does not hold up, letting your own test cases settle the question. HAB is on the assignment tier deliberately: this is the one tier where you may use AI, so learning to catch a confident machine when it is wrong is part of the training, not a distraction from it.

The story is a spine, not a cage. The technical ask is always clear and testable, and you can earn full marks with the plot skipped entirely. The mission never hides what you have to build.

## How an Assignment Works

Each regular assignment is one problem, worked in a fixed order:

- **Design your acceptance tests.** Before writing any code, you decide what correct behavior looks like: a set of test cases, including the boundary and tricky ones, each with the answer worked out by hand. We give you one worked example; you write the rest.
- **Write your pseudocode.** A plain-language plan of how your code turns its inputs into its answer, naming the intermediate values you will create.
- **Write your code.** The functions themselves, or, before the Functions lecture, a short program that prints the answer, checked for correctness by an automatic grader.
- **A paper practice**, one tracing or diagram exercise done by hand and photographed, in the same format the exams use, with the worked solution hidden on the page so you attempt it first and then check yourself.
- **A reflection**, two sentences on what you used help for, including whether and how you used AI.
- **An optional summit problem** on most assignments, a harder stretch that earns extra credit.

You submit in two places: everything that is not code, meaning your test cases, your pseudocode, your paper trace, and your reflection, goes in a single **PDF on Canvas**, and your **code goes to Gradescope**. Each assignment's page lists exactly what to hand in where and carries its own grading rubric.

Every assignment is solvable using only the language features the course has taught by the week it is due, so you are never blocked by something you have not seen yet. The [Language Ladder](/reference/language-ladder/) lists what that is, as a recommendation you can lean on when you are unsure; you are always free to use a feature you already know. See the [Schedule](/overview/schedule/) for which recitation each assignment prepares you for, and read [Running and Submitting Your Code](/practicalities/starter-repo-and-check/) for the mechanics of the starter repository and how to submit.

## The Chapters

The assignments run in mission order. Start with the systems check, then follow the mission from the pad to Sol 100.

1. [Systems Checkout](/assignments/field-office-setup/): bring your tools online before the mission begins.
2. [Pre-Flight Check](/assignments/household-energy-panel/): read the instrument panel before the launch window opens.
3. [The Launch Window](/assignments/ev-carbon-payback/): the countdown and fuel numbers for launch.
4. [The Injection Burn](/assignments/low-carbon-concrete/): the burn that sends the crew toward Mars.
5. [Transit Checks](/assignments/burn-window-clearance/): the interlocks that keep the crew safe in transit.
6. [The Airlock and the Readings](/assignments/sensor-drift-check/): validating the readings at the airlock.
7. [Flight Qualification](/assignments/dashboard-publication-tests/): testing the software before it is trusted.
8. [The First Drive](/assignments/solar-array-lifetime/): the rover's first drive across the surface.
9. [The Comms Window](/assignments/field-logger-codes/): encoding and decoding messages home.
10. [The Sol 100 Report](/assignments/emissions-inventory/): the capstone status report, one hundred sols in.

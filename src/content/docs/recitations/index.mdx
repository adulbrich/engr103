The recitations are where you show, in class and on your own, that you can do the work you practiced at home. You have already met each week's problem family in that week's assignment, with any tool you liked, including AI. A recitation is the same kind of problem worked in the room, on your own, so a student who did the assignment honestly should walk in ready. That is the whole point of the tier: it is what makes practicing for real worth your while, and it keeps a high-stakes session from turning into a surprise.

## Rubber Duck Robotics

Where the assignments follow the grounded, real-stakes work of the Willamette Resource Office, the recitations are set at **Rubber Duck Robotics**, a mediocre but lovable novelty-gadget company: self-stirring mugs, motivational bathtub ducks, duck-race timers, the occasional smart hot tub. In the room you are the new firmware intern, and each recitation is a **ticket from Gary**, the senior engineer, handing you the gadget work for the week. The comic tone is deliberate: a high-stakes session is stressful enough, and a novelty-mug problem worth points is still worth points, so the jokes keep the nerves from swamping the work.

## How a Session Works

Every session has the same shape:

- **A warm-up**, one direct application of the week's family that everyone should finish.
- **A couple of quick checks**, short multiple-choice questions on the week's ideas.
- **Two core problems**, fresh variations of the skeletons you practiced on the assignment: new values, new Rubber Duck costume, at most one small twist, never new territory. One of the two usually has you reading code (tracing it, predicting its output, or finding its bug) and the other has you writing a short function or fragment; you never write a whole program in a recitation, because that is what the assignments are for. The warm-up, the quick checks, and the two core problems are the whole session, so a prepared intern can finish it comfortably.
- **An extra-credit problem**, one harder ticket that sometimes reaches into territory you have not practiced, because working engineers face exactly that. It always carries the same promise: attempting it can only help you, never hurt you.

You work the whole session by hand, and you may answer in Python, in Rust, or in a mix; a correct answer in either language earns full marks. Where a problem shows code, it shows the same program in both languages, and you read whichever you prefer.

The problems are stated straight, with no tricks and nothing to catch: they are an honest check that the practiced work transferred. The nine graded sessions run from week 2 to week 10, and the lowest one is dropped; week 1 is an ungraded onboarding session that gets you ready for the rest.

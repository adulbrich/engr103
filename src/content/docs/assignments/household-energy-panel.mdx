The Willamette Resource Office runs free home energy audits for residents who request one. Today's audit covers one appliance: an electric water heater, a 4500 watt heating element with a 50 gallon tank, that a field technician timed running an average of 2 hours a day. The office's report template needs those numbers turned into a panel of readings before the audit goes out. You have only just learned how a program stores and combines values, so the panel program is handed to you nearly complete: your job is to fill in the four missing readings. AURA has offered its own shortcut for one of them, so check that before it goes on the report.

## How This Assignment Works

You work **test first**, the way the office's tools are really built: you read the problem, decide what correct output looks like by writing your own test rows, plan the program in pseudocode, and only then finish the code. You have not learned variables or functions yet, so the panel program comes as a **complete, runnable file with marked `TODO` lines**; you replace each placeholder with the literal or arithmetic expression that produces the reading described. The automated grader runs your program and compares its printed output, line by line, against the expected output. Solve it in Python or Rust, whichever you prefer; both are checked against the same panel.

Two rules hold for every reading here, and both come straight from the [Data Representation and Memory](/lectures/data-representation/) lecture. First, an expression may not mix a whole number with a decimal number: Rust refuses to compile `3 + 2.5`, so any reading that needs a fraction must be written with **every** number as a float (for example `100.0`, not `100`). Second, to get a real fraction from division you must divide floats, because dividing two whole numbers is a separate rule you have not met yet.

## The Problem

The energy panel prints one reading per line, in a fixed order. The label is already written for you; you fill in the four readings below it.

The grid's carbon intensity is a real, cited figure: **275 grams of CO2e per kilowatt-hour**, the total output emission rate for the NWPP (Northwest Power Pool) subregion, which covers most of Oregon and Washington (EPA eGRID2022, released January 2024, rounded from 605.9 lb CO2e/MWh). The office's own benchmarking survey lists the state's median household annual electricity use as **10,400 kWh**.

Your finished program must print exactly five lines, in this order:

1. The panel label, the text `ENERGY AUDIT` (already written).
2. The water heater's annual energy use in kilowatt-hours: its rated power, `4500` watts, running for `2.0` hours a day, scaled up over a full year. This is a decimal number, so write every number in the expression as a float.
3. The water heater's annual emissions in kilograms of CO2e: the annual energy use from the line above, at the grid's carbon intensity of `275` grams of CO2e per kilowatt-hour. The intensity is given in grams; the panel reports kilograms. This is also a decimal number.
4. Whether this household's total annual use, a meter reading of `11,000` kWh, sits above the state median given above. This is a boolean.
5. The smart meter's status bitfield: a connectivity flag worth `0b100` (the meter's last radio check-in succeeded), plus a two-bit retry-count field worth `0b10` per retry, with `3` retries logged this billing period, minus a diagnostic-clear flag worth `0b1` for the meter's one passed self-test. Write the three codes as binary literals and combine them with arithmetic.

Do not type the final numbers directly. Every numeric reading must be written as the arithmetic expression that produces it, not as the number it comes out to. The boolean reading is the one exception: comparing two numbers with `>` is not something you have learned yet, so you work out by hand which way the comparison goes and write the boolean literal it produces.

Keep whole numbers and decimals apart within a single expression. The meter status line mixes addition, subtraction, and multiplication, so the order those operators run in decides its answer.

## Step 1: Write Your Acceptance Tests

Do not use `assert` yet; a table of rows is what this step wants. Work out each expected value by hand from the requirements, not by running the program.

Your table must include, and label, at least these rows:

- The annual emissions, reduced fully by hand from your annual-energy row and the grid's carbon intensity, including the gram-to-kilogram conversion.

## Paper Practice

Reduce this expression by hand, on paper, one operator at a time, and write the single value it comes out to. It is all whole numbers, and it includes a binary literal and a set of parentheses, the same precedence and literal rules the panel readings turn on.

```text title="Reduce this expression"
5 * 6 - 3 * (0b111 - 2) + 8
```

<Reveal label="Reveal the reduction">
```text title="Reducing 5 * 6 - 3 * (0b111 - 2) + 8"
5 * 6 - 3 * (0b111 - 2) + 8
5 * 6 - 3 * (7 - 2) + 8      (0b111 is the whole number 7)
5 * 6 - 3 * 5 + 8            (the parentheses reduce first: 7 - 2 is 5)
30 - 15 + 8                  (both products form before the + and -: 5 * 6 is 30, 3 * 5 is 15)
23                           (left to right: 30 - 15 is 15, then 15 + 8 is 23)
```

Parentheses reduce first, then multiplication and division before addition and subtraction, and operators of equal standing run left to right.
</Reveal>

Photograph your reduction and include it in your PDF on Canvas. This part is graded on completion, not correctness, so reduce it yourself first, then open the reveal to check.

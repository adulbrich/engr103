Sol 100 has arrived: the habitat's hundredth full day on the Martian surface, and Mission Control wants a complete status report, not just another routine readout. All day, the environmental system has been checking the cabin's internal air pressure and handing each reading to flight software as it comes in. Mission Control also logged that the habitat's solar arrays produced 4 percent more power than yesterday's report, a bright note for the log but not a number your functions need. Two jobs fall to you as the flight-software crew member on shift: record each good reading into the sol's running manifest as it arrives, and, at the end of the sol, archive that manifest into a permanent copy for the mission log that Sol 101 can never disturb.

## How This Assignment Works

You work **test first**, the way flight software is really written: you read the problem, decide what correct behavior looks like by writing your own test cases, plan the code in pseudocode, and only then write the code. The problem is solvable in Python or Rust, whichever you prefer. The program's `main`, its console input and output, and the local test runner are already written; you write the two functions named below and nothing else, and you may work each in a different language. The two functions have two different contracts, so the grader checks them two different ways. For `archive_manifest`, which returns a value, the grader reads what you `return`, then records one more reading into the original manifest and confirms the list you returned did not change with it. For `record_reading`, which takes the manifest by reference and has nothing meaningful to return, the grader calls your function and then inspects the caller's own list afterward, exactly the way the [Sharing and Mutation](/lectures/sharing-and-mutation/) lecture showed a function change a list its caller kept. The order is fixed: design your tests, write your pseudocode, then write your functions. After that comes an optional summit stretch, a short paper trace, a one-page accessibility and equity critique, and a two-sentence reflection.

## The Problem

The cabin is built to hold its internal air pressure between `95` and `105` kilopascals, and only a reading inside that band belongs in the sol's record. Two things have to happen with the day's readings. As each reading arrives, flight software checks it against the safe range and, if it belongs, records it into one running list for the sol, the manifest, which the rest of the report reads from as it grows. Then, once the sol is closed out, the report needs a permanent, separate copy of that manifest for the mission log, a copy that stays exactly as Sol 100 left it no matter what Sol 101 later appends to the live manifest.

These are two independent jobs, and you write one function for each.

1. `record_reading(manifest, reading, low, high)` must append `reading` to the end of `manifest` when `reading` is within the safe range (greater than or equal to `low` and less than or equal to `high`), and must leave `manifest` completely unchanged when `reading` is outside that range. It does not return a value; the caller's `manifest` is what changes, and the grader inspects `manifest` after the call.
2. `archive_manifest(manifest)` must build and return a new list holding the same readings currently in `manifest`, in the same order. The returned list must be independent of `manifest`: any reading recorded into `manifest` after the call must never appear in the list you already returned.

Both ends of the safe range count as inside it. How each function does its job, and what intermediate values you give names to along the way, is entirely your decision.

We give you **one** worked example to show the format. Starting from a `manifest` holding `[100, 98]`:

| Call | Result |
|---|---|
| `record_reading(manifest, 102, 95, 105)` | `manifest` becomes `[100, 98, 102]` (reading in range, appended) |
| `archive_manifest(manifest)` | returns `[100, 98, 102]`, a separate list; recording another reading into `manifest` afterward leaves that returned list at `[100, 98, 102]` |

**Assumptions and edge cases.** `manifest` may already hold any number of readings, including none. `reading`, `low`, and `high` are whole numbers, and `low` is guaranteed to be no greater than `high`. For `record_reading`, handle both boundary readings (exactly `low` and exactly `high` are recorded) and both directions of being out of range (the manifest is left untouched). For `archive_manifest`, handle the empty manifest: the returned archive is an empty list, and it must stay empty even after the live manifest later gains a reading.

## Step 1: Write Your Acceptance Tests

Before writing any code, decide what correct behavior looks like. Write a set of test cases for your two functions as a plain table of rows. Do not use `assert`; a table of rows is what this step wants. Because the two functions have two different contracts, your rows look different for each:

- For `record_reading`, each row is the `manifest` before the call, the call itself with its `reading`, `low`, and `high`, and the `manifest` you expect afterward. Work out each expected list by hand from the safe-range rule.
- For `archive_manifest`, each row is the `manifest` before the call, the call, the list you expect it to return, and the `manifest` after you record one more reading into it, so the row can show whether the returned archive stayed put.

Your table must include, and label, at least these cases:

- For `record_reading`: a normal in-range reading appended, both boundary readings (exactly `low` and exactly `high`, both recorded), an out-of-range reading that leaves the manifest unchanged, and a case where `manifest` starts empty.
- For `archive_manifest`: a normal archive of a manifest holding several readings, an **independence** case that records a reading into the original manifest after archiving and shows the returned archive did not change, and a case where `manifest` starts empty.
- At least one case, for either function, whose expected result you work out fully by hand.

Think about what HAB claimed while you do this: a good independence case would catch an archive that turned out to be just another name for the live manifest.

<WhatDiffers>
<Fragment slot="differs">
The two languages disagree on how a list reaches a function. In Python a list is shared the moment you pass it, so `record_reading` changes the caller's `manifest` with a plain `.append` and no annotation anywhere, and `archive_manifest` builds and returns a new list of its own. Rust makes the same two intentions explicit in the types: the in-place function takes `manifest: &mut Vec<i32>` and is called as `&mut manifest`, while the copy function takes `manifest: &Vec<i32>` and returns an owned `Vec<i32>` it built itself. Same two behaviors either way; only how each language spells the sharing differs.

## Paper Practice

Trace this program by hand, on paper. It is the aliasing pattern from the [Sharing and Mutation](/lectures/sharing-and-mutation/) lecture: `shadow` is set to `primary`, which gives the one list a second name rather than making a second list. Build a variable table with a row for each line, tracking what `primary` and `shadow` each hold after that line runs. Then write the exact two values the program prints.

```python title="trace_me.py"
def add_sample(readings, value):
    readings.append(value)

primary = [100, 98]
shadow = primary
add_sample(primary, 101)
print(primary)
print(shadow)
```

<Reveal label="Reveal the completed trace">
The line `shadow = primary` does not copy the list; it makes `shadow` a second name for the very same list object, so anything that changes one is seen through the other.

| Line | `primary` after | `shadow` after |
|---|---|---|
| `primary = [100, 98]` | `[100, 98]` | (not yet bound) |
| `shadow = primary` | `[100, 98]` | `[100, 98]` (same list, second name) |
| `add_sample(primary, 101)` | `[100, 98, 101]` | `[100, 98, 101]` |

`add_sample` appends `101` to the one shared list, so the change shows through both names. The program prints `[100, 98, 101]`, then `[100, 98, 101]` again. Rust prints a `Vec<i32>` the same way, `[100, 98, 101]`, with no display difference here since every value is a whole number, never a float.
</Reveal>

Photograph your trace and include it in your PDF on Canvas. This part is graded on completion (the attached photograph), not on whether every cell is right, so attempt it by hand first, then open the reveal to check yourself.

## Accessibility and Equity Critique

This part is not code. Write about one page (roughly 300 to 500 words) critiquing the accessibility and equity limitations of a real computational tool you actually use or have encountered, something people rely on today: a phone app, a website, a piece of assistive technology, a search engine, a hiring or screening algorithm, a fitness tracker, a voice assistant, a mapping app, a school or workplace platform. Pick a real, specific tool, not a hypothetical one and not anything from the Mission Ares story.

Your one page must address all three of these:

1. **Who is the tool built around.** Describe the "default" user the tool seems to assume: their abilities, their language, their income, their hardware, their situation. Be specific about what leads you to that description, drawn from how the tool actually behaves.
2. **Who it leaves out, and how.** Name one concrete accessibility or equity limitation: a group of people the tool underserves, excludes, or actively harms, and the specific mechanism, a design choice, an assumption baked into the software, a cost, a requirement, that produces that limitation. Vague statements like "it could be more inclusive" are not enough; name the mechanism.
3. **What closing the gap would take.** Describe one real change that would narrow the limitation you named, and name the trade-off it carries, more engineering effort, added cost, a slower feature, a different design entirely. A fix with no trade-off at all is a sign you have not looked closely enough.

This critique is graded on completion, evaluated on whether it genuinely engages with all three points about a real tool, not on whether the instructor agrees with your conclusions. It is not shared with anyone but the instructor.

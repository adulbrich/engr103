The trans-Mars injection burn is the long engine firing that leaves Earth orbit and puts the crew on course for Mars. It fires in two stages, one engine after the other, and the flight plan has to decide how many seconds each stage burns. Two things must come out exactly right: the two stages together have to add up to the total change in speed the trajectory needs, and together they have to use exactly the fuel budgeted for the burn. Your job as the new flight-software crew member is to write the code that computes each stage's burn time.

## How This Assignment Works

You work **test first**, the way flight software is really written: you read the problem, decide what correct behavior looks like by writing your own test cases, plan the code in pseudocode, and only then write the code. The problem is solvable in Python or Rust, whichever you prefer. The equations you need are given below in full, so you never derive or solve anything by hand; the work is turning them into clear, correct code. The order is fixed: design your tests, write your pseudocode, then write your functions. After that comes an optional extra-credit stretch, a short paper trace, and a two-sentence reflection.

## What to Submit

You submit in two places. Mechanics are in [Running and Submitting Your Code](/practicalities/starter-repo-and-check/); each section below also repeats what it asks for.

- **A single PDF on Canvas** holding everything that is not code: your **acceptance tests**, your **pseudocode**, your **paper trace**, and your **reflection**.
- **Your code on Gradescope**: the two functions `stage_one_seconds` and `stage_two_seconds`, plus the extra-credit `first_stage_seconds` if you attempt the summit.

## The Problem

Stage one burns for <Latex formula="t_1" /> seconds and stage two for <Latex formula="t_2" /> seconds; these two burn times are what you need to find. Each stage has two known rates. Stage one adds speed at <Latex formula="v_1" /> metres per second for every second it burns and consumes fuel at <Latex formula="f_1" /> kilograms per second; stage two adds speed at <Latex formula="v_2" /> and burns fuel at <Latex formula="f_2" />. The whole burn must deliver a total change in speed of <Latex formula="D" /> metres per second and use exactly <Latex formula="F" /> kilograms of fuel. The two engines together weigh 1,800 kilograms dry.

Those two requirements are two equations. The speed each stage adds is its rate times its burn time, and the two must sum to <Latex formula="D" />; the fuel each stage uses is its rate times its burn time, and the two must sum to <Latex formula="F" />:

<Latex formula="v_1\,t_1 + v_2\,t_2 = D" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="f_1\,t_1 + f_2\,t_2 = F" inline={false} />

Mission Control has already solved this pair of equations for the two burn times. Writing <Latex formula="\Delta" /> for a shared value that appears in both results, the burn times are:

<Latex formula="\Delta = v_1 f_2 - v_2 f_1" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="t_1 = \frac{D\,f_2 - v_2\,F}{\Delta}" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="t_2 = \frac{v_1\,F - D\,f_1}{\Delta}" inline={false} />

You will write two functions:

1. `stage_one_seconds(v1, v2, f1, f2, total_dv, fuel_budget)` returns <Latex formula="t_1" />, stage one's burn time in seconds.
2. `stage_two_seconds(v1, v2, f1, f2, total_dv, fuel_budget)` returns <Latex formula="t_2" />, stage two's burn time in seconds.

Both return a float. Here `total_dv` is <Latex formula="D" /> and `fuel_budget` is <Latex formula="F" />. How you compute each formula, and what intermediate values you give names to along the way, is entirely your decision.

We give you **one** worked example to show the format. With `v1 = 5.0`, `v2 = 8.0`, `f1 = 3.0`, `f2 = 2.0`, `total_dv = 1240.0`, and `fuel_budget = 520.0`:

| Call | Returns |
|---|---|
| `stage_one_seconds(5.0, 8.0, 3.0, 2.0, 1240.0, 520.0)` | `120.0` |
| `stage_two_seconds(5.0, 8.0, 3.0, 2.0, 1240.0, 520.0)` | `80.0` |

**Inputs and guarantees.** Every input is a float greater than zero. The two stages always have different performance, so <Latex formula="\Delta" /> is never zero and you do not have to guard against dividing by it. Mission Control only plans burns that are physically possible, so both burn times come out greater than zero. Note that <Latex formula="\Delta" /> itself can come out negative, and the formulas still give the right answer when it does.

## Summit Problem (extra credit)

This one is a real stretch, and it earns extra credit. It is also your practice for the graded stretch problem in the recitation room, where a harder problem is waiting.

Some injection burns fire in **three** stages, not two, and then a third requirement is added: the whole burn must also last an exact number of seconds. With stage burn times <Latex formula="t_1, t_2, t_3" />, speed rates <Latex formula="v_1, v_2, v_3" />, fuel rates <Latex formula="f_1, f_2, f_3" />, a total speed target <Latex formula="D" />, a fuel budget <Latex formula="F" />, and a total burn time <Latex formula="T" />, the three requirements are three equations:

<Latex formula="v_1 t_1 + v_2 t_2 + v_3 t_3 = D" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="f_1 t_1 + f_2 t_2 + f_3 t_3 = F" inline={false} />

<Latex formula="t_1 + t_2 + t_3 = T" inline={false} />

One standard way to solve a three-equation system like this is **Cramer's rule**, which is given here for you to implement. First, the value of a three-by-three determinant is:

<Latex formula="\det\begin{pmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{pmatrix} = a(ei - fh) - b(di - fg) + c(dh - eg)" inline={false} />

Let <Latex formula="\Delta" /> be the determinant of the grid of coefficients on the left-hand sides (the three <Latex formula="v" /> values on the first row, the three <Latex formula="f" /> values on the second, and `1, 1, 1` on the third). Let <Latex formula="\Delta_1" /> be that same determinant but with the first column replaced by the three right-hand-side values <Latex formula="D, F, T" />. Then stage one's burn time is:

<Latex formula="t_1 = \frac{\Delta_1}{\Delta}" inline={false} />

Write `first_stage_seconds`, returning <Latex formula="t_1" />:

## Paper Practice

Trace this program by hand, on paper. It uses nested function calls, the pattern from the [Functions](/lectures/functions/) lecture. The transfer pump moves propellant out of a tank in two steps. Build a table: for each call to `litres_left`, write the values its `total` and `used` parameters hold and what it returns; then, for `remaining_after_two`, write the values bound to `step` and to `result`. Write the exact value the program prints.

```python title="trace_me.py"
def litres_left(total, used):
    return total - used

def remaining_after_two(total, first, second):
    step = litres_left(total, first)
    result = litres_left(step, second)
    return result

answer = remaining_after_two(100.0, 40.0, 35.0)
print(answer)
```

<Reveal label="Reveal the completed trace">
The call `remaining_after_two(100.0, 40.0, 35.0)` runs `litres_left(100.0, 40.0)` first, which returns `60.0` and is bound to `step`; then `litres_left(60.0, 35.0)`, which returns `25.0` and is bound to `result`.

| Line | call | `total` | `used` | `step` | `result` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `step = litres_left(total, first)` | `litres_left(100.0, 40.0)` | `100.0` | `40.0` | `60.0` | |
| `result = litres_left(step, second)` | `litres_left(60.0, 35.0)` | `60.0` | `35.0` | `60.0` | `25.0` |

The program prints `25.0`. That trailing `.0` is Python's display of a whole-valued float; the same program written in Rust prints the `f64` as `25`.
</Reveal>
